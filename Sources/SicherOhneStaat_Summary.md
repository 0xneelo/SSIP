# Summary: "Sicher ohne Staat" by Oliver Janich

> **Full Title**: *Sicher ohne Staat: Wie eine natürliche Rechtsordnung ohne Gewaltmonopol funktioniert*  
> **English**: *Secure Without the State: How a Natural Legal Order Functions Without a Monopoly on Violence*  
> **Author**: Oliver Janich  
> **Note**: This summary captures key concepts for the SSIP whitepaper. For the full text, please purchase the book.

## Overview

Janich's book presents a comprehensive framework for a **Private Law Society (Privatrechtsordnung/PRG)** - a society where security, justice, and all governance functions are provided by competing private entities rather than a state monopoly. The book addresses common objections and provides economic analysis of why such a system would be more efficient, peaceful, and just.

---

## Part I: Foundational Principles

### Chapter 1: The Core Axiom

**"The judge must not be a party to the conflict."**

This is the fundamental logical axiom from which all other conclusions derive:

- When the state is both rulemaker AND enforcer, it is inherently a party to every conflict
- A neutral third party must adjudicate disputes
- The state violates this principle by definition - it writes laws, enforces them, AND judges violations of them
- This creates an irresolvable conflict of interest

**Implications:**
- State courts cannot be neutral when the state is a party
- Tax disputes are judged by state courts (conflict of interest)
- Regulatory violations are judged by state agencies (conflict of interest)
- The only solution: competing private arbitration with no monopoly power

### Chapter 2: Why Security is a Market Good

**Key Arguments:**

1. **Security is a service** - like any other, it can be priced, competed for, and improved
2. **No "public goods" exception** - the economic arguments for state provision fail:
   - Free-rider problem is manageable through contracts
   - Economies of scale favor large private providers, not monopolies
   - Quality improves with competition

3. **Price discovery** - Without market prices, resources cannot be efficiently allocated
   - How many police per citizen? The state cannot know
   - Private firms discover optimal ratios through competition

### Chapter 3: The Non-Aggression Principle (NAP)

**Definition**: No person may initiate force against another person's body or property.

**Key Points:**
- NAP is the foundational rule of a PLS
- All contracts must be NAP-compliant
- Defensive force is permitted; aggressive force is not
- The state inherently violates NAP through taxation (coerced payment for unchosen services)

**Enforcement Mechanism:**
- Private Security Agencies (PSAs) enforce NAP
- Violations trigger insurance claims and restitution
- Repeat offenders face escalating consequences
- Ultimate sanction: loss of insurance coverage (social/economic exclusion)

## Part II: How Private Security Works

### Chapter 4: Private Security Agencies (PSAs)

**Business Model:**
- Citizens pay premiums for protection services
- PSAs compete on:
  - Response time
  - Prevention rate
  - Price
  - Customer satisfaction
  - NAP compliance record

**Key Insight**: PSAs have **economic incentives** to:
- Prevent crime (cheaper than responding)
- Resolve disputes peacefully (cheaper than conflict)
- Maintain reputation (customer retention)
- Avoid false accusations (liability)

**Comparison to State Police:**

| Factor | State Police | Private Security Agency |
|--------|-------------|------------------------|
| Funding | Forced taxation | Voluntary premiums |
| Accountability | Political | Market (customers leave) |
| Incentive | Budget maximization | Cost efficiency + quality |
| Response to failure | Demand more funding | Lose customers, go bankrupt |
| Innovation | Bureaucratic resistance | Competitive advantage |

### Chapter 5: Private Courts and Arbitration

**Structure:**
- Multiple competing court systems
- Citizens pre-select arbiters in contracts
- Different legal frameworks available (Common Law, Civil Code, religious law, etc.)
- Judges stake reputation and capital

**Quality Assurance:**
- Appeal systems across court networks
- Judges with high overturn rates lose business
- Transparent track records
- Financial penalties for corrupt rulings

**Key Insight**: When citizens choose their arbiters, courts compete on:
- Fairness (both parties must agree to the arbiter)
- Speed (faster resolution = more business)
- Cost (efficient proceedings)
- Expertise (specialized knowledge)

### Chapter 6: The Insurance Layer

**Central Role of Insurance:**
- Insurance companies are the "regulators" of a PLS
- They set standards, verify compliance, price risk
- Bad actors become uninsurable → economic exclusion

**How it Works:**
1. Citizen purchases protection insurance
2. If victimized, insurance pays immediate restitution
3. Insurance pursues perpetrator for recovery
4. Perpetrator's insurance pays if they're covered
5. Uninsured perpetrators face direct enforcement

**Risk Pricing:**
- Higher-risk individuals pay higher premiums
- Incentivizes good behavior
- Gradual rehabilitation through premium reduction
- Similar to auto insurance "safe driver" discounts

## Part III: Addressing Common Objections

### Chapter 7: "What About the Poor?"

**Arguments:**

1. **Security is cheap** - Real-world examples:
   - Oakland crowdfunding: $0.50/day/citizen
   - Sidney Torres (New Orleans): Dramatic crime reduction at low cost
   - Gated communities: Fraction of tax burden

2. **Without state overhead**, protection costs plummet:
   - No military adventurism
   - No bureaucratic waste
   - Competition drives efficiency

3. **Charity would expand** - Currently suppressed by:
   - High taxes leaving less for donations
   - State "crowding out" private charity
   - Illusion that "someone else handles it"

4. **Community/family support** - Historical norm before welfare state

### Chapter 8: "What About Warlords/Mafia?"

**Why Monopoly Violence is Unstable in PLS:**

1. **No territory to hold** - Value is in customer relationships, not land
2. **United opposition** - All other PSAs and citizens oppose aggressors
3. **No tax base** - Aggressor must fund operations from shrinking revenue
4. **Employee defection** - Security workers prefer honest employment
5. **Reputation destruction** - Instant loss of all legitimate business

**The Mafia Problem:**

Janich argues the Mafia **requires** the state:
- Profits from prohibited goods (drugs, gambling, prostitution)
- In PLS, these are legal → no black market premium
- Mafia must corrupt ONE monopoly court system
- In PLS, must corrupt ALL competing courts (impossible)
- Only remaining business: protection rackets → easily prosecuted

**Key Insight**: "The Mafia is the state's bastard child. Remove the state, and the Mafia has no ecological niche."

### Chapter 9: "What About Defense Against States?"

**Strategies:**

1. **Deterrence through targeting**:
   - PSAs would target aggressive leaders, not populations
   - Offer: "Convert to PLS and get a board seat" OR "Continue and face elimination"
   - Economic calculation favors negotiation

2. **Unoccupiable territory**:
   - Armed citizenry (those who choose)
   - No centralized infrastructure to seize
   - No tax apparatus to capture
   - Guerrilla resistance by definition

3. **Historical examples**:
   - Switzerland (armed neutrality)
   - Liechtenstein, Monaco (low-tax, never invaded)
   - Afghanistan (unconquerable even by superpowers)

4. **No plausible motive**:
   - What would invaders gain?
   - No tax revenue to capture
   - No population willing to submit
   - Massive resistance guaranteed

### Chapter 10: "What About Externalities?"

**Environmental Protection:**

- Pollution violates NAP (harming others' property/health)
- Victims can sue polluters directly
- Class action suits against major polluters
- Insurance companies refuse coverage for polluters
- No regulatory capture by industries

**Key Insight**: State environmental agencies are routinely captured by industries they regulate. Private enforcement has no such vulnerability.

## Part IV: The No-Go Area Problem

### Chapter 11: Cleaning Up Lawless Zones

**The Business Case:**

1. **Huge profit potential** - Winning customers in underserved areas
2. **Specialization** - Some firms focus on high-risk markets
3. **Progressive expansion** - "Go areas" spread as crime falls

**Operational Approach:**

1. Insurance offers coverage to residents willing to pay
2. Specialized response teams extract victims, apprehend criminals
3. Anonymous courts process perpetrators (protecting judges)
4. Criminals held at undisclosed locations (preventing gang rescue)
5. Success attracts more customers → funding for expansion

**Contrast with State:**
- Police withdraw from no-go areas (nothing to gain)
- Crimes go unreported in statistics
- Politicians deny problem exists
- No economic incentive to solve

**The 24-Hour Ultimatum:**
- After demonstrating capability, PSA offers gangs choice:
  - Leave the area
  - Become legitimate and get insurance (expensive, supervised)
  - Face enforcement

## Part V: Unemployment and Social Security

### Chapter 12: Why Welfare Creates Dependency

**The State's Trick:**
- Taxes extracted invisibly (payroll deduction, VAT)
- If paid in lump sum annually, revolution would occur
- Average burden: ~70% of income in Germany

**Private Alternatives:**

1. **Personal savings** - Without taxes, 3 years of work = 10 years of savings
2. **Family support** - Natural first line of defense
3. **Friendly societies/unions** - Historical providers of welfare
4. **Charity** - Expands when taxes fall
5. **Insurance** - Disability, unemployment coverage available

**The Key Difference:**
- State welfare: No accountability, moral hazard, dependency
- Private charity: Personal knowledge, social pressure, temporary support

**Personal Example (from book):**
- Author housed unemployed friend
- Friend made excuses for available jobs
- Author confronted him directly
- Friend found work within days
- This accountability is impossible for state bureaucracies

## Part VI: Technology and the Future

### Chapter 13: Why States Suppress Innovation

**Mechanisms:**
1. **Patents** - State learns about invention, can classify as "national security"
2. **Direct suppression** - Inventors imprisoned or killed
3. **Regulatory capture** - Incumbents use regulations to block competitors

**Examples Cited:**
- Wilhelm Reich (forced to destroy own inventions)
- Nikola Tesla (sabotaged by J.P. Morgan)
- Prof. Claus Turtur (denied funding for energy research)
- Numerous "suicides" of alternative energy inventors

**In PLS:**
- Inventors can insure against assassination
- No patent system to expose inventions to state
- Market demand drives innovation adoption
- No regulatory barriers to entry

**Key Insight**: "The abolition of the state would catapult humanity forward by 100 years technologically."

### Chapter 14: The End of Propaganda

**Why Media Manipulation Becomes Irrelevant:**

1. **No political power to capture** - Elections don't exist
2. **Consumer sovereignty** - Can't force purchases based on propaganda
3. **Competition** - Lies exposed by competitors
4. **No public funding** - State broadcasters disappear

**Current Media Problems:**
- Intelligence agency infiltration (documented)
- Fiat money allows bank ownership of media
- State funding creates dependence
- Political advertising distorts coverage

**In PLS:**
- Media can still lie, but consequences are minimal
- No policies to vote for based on propaganda
- Individual choice remains intact
- Market punishes unreliable sources

## Part VII: The Minimal State Fallacy

### Chapter 15: Why "Limited Government" Always Fails

**The Iron Law:**
- People seek their own advantage
- Those with power want more power
- EVERY state expands over time

**Evidence:**
- US Constitution: Most limited government ever designed
- Result: Aggressive police state, global military empire
- Took ~200 years, but expansion was inevitable

**The Logical Problem:**
- Minimal statists accept market superiority for most goods
- But exempt security arbitrarily
- No principled reason for the exception
- Same economic laws apply to security

**Roland Baader's Admission** (prominent minimal statist):
- Acknowledged anarchists have better arguments
- His preference was "gut feeling"
- Fear of the unknown, not logic

## Part VIII: Real-World Evidence

### Chapter 16: Private Security Today

**New Orleans (Sidney Torres):**
- App for crime reporting
- Private response team: 2-minute average
- State police: 28-minute average
- Crime rates dropped dramatically

**Oakland Crowdfunding:**
- Citizens funded private patrols
- Cost: $0.50/day/citizen
- Crime fell 50% in three years

**Starret City (Brooklyn):**
- 20,000 residents, private security
- Located in worst crime district
- Crime rate: 6.57/1000 (vs 49.86 surrounding area)

**Global Statistics:**
- USA: 3x more private security than state police
- Norway, Estonia: More private than state security
- Trend is accelerating

### Chapter 17: The 98% Principle

**Key Statistic:**
- Only 2% of citizens commit crimes annually
- 98% have incentive for effective law enforcement
- Market responds to 98%, not 2%

**Implication:**
- Private security serves the law-abiding majority
- No structural incentive to protect criminals
- Democratic states often protect criminal minorities for votes
