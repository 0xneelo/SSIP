# CHAPTER 2: THE TRUE ROOT OF THE PROBLEM

Whoever wants to solve a problem must recognize the cause. It is important to really get to the core of the matter. Whoever does not recognize the true cause always treats only symptoms. It will surprise some libertarians that I do not see the true cause of social and economic problems in the violation of property rights, the non-aggression principle, or natural law.

The disregard of these principles is only the consequence of the violation of another principle. When we discover this principle, we have discovered the root cause, the real root of the problem. When we know the root cause, all problems can be traced back to this principle. If this is not successful, we have not found the root cause at all.

**The real root cause can be summed up in six simple words:**

> ## *"The conflict resolver must not be a party to the conflict."*

In other words: **The one who decides a conflict, i.e., the judge, must not himself be part of the conflict.**

This is immediately obvious. Imagine you believe a company sold you a defective product. If it takes the product back out of goodwill or insight, no conflict arises. If it refuses to refund your money, it would obviously be nonsensical if the company were allowed to decide who is right.

**The state is by definition the ultimate judge in all legal matters, including those concerning itself. This cannot possibly work.** You cannot, for example, sue with the goal of getting your taxes back if politicians do not keep their election promises, or if you generally believe that the state does not deliver what you expect from it.

It was **Hans-Hermann Hoppe**, for me the most important living philosopher and one of the greatest in history, who discovered this principle as the basis for libertarianism and brilliantly worked it out. Most anarchists recognize this principle but are often under the mistaken impression that we are dealing here only with an extension of libertarianism or the Austrian School. It would be just another arrow in the quiver that could be shot at state lovers.

**In reality, Hoppe has discovered something completely new that has raised the theory to a completely new level.** The Austrian School remains useful for describing the consequences of restricted property rights. However, this principle is independent of the Austrian School or libertarianism and precedes property rights.

I will try to prove this to you logically:

## There Is No Property Under the State

In a state, there is no property. There is only property by the grace of the state, so-called fiat property. Not only does the state levy taxes, it can also take away already guaranteed property rights at any time and does so regularly. For example, it determines that the owner of an apartment may not leave it empty, perhaps because it wants to house so-called refugees in it. The state even presumes to speak of "misappropriation" when you rent your own apartment to tourists. In Munich, this carries a penalty of 500,000 euros. The state can introduce a wealth tax or even the taxation of air — see the proposals for CO2 tax — from one day to the next.

From this it logically follows that **either there is no property at all, or only when there is no state.** I will call this state, the ultimate decider or ultimate judge for conflicts, the **violence monopolist** in the following. The term is not entirely precise because the citizen may, for example, use violence in self-defense, but the state ultimately decides whether this use of force was lawful. Therefore, I use this term because it is common and not as unwieldy as "Ultimate Judge in a Certain Territory."

I will show that property actually exists. Then only one logical conclusion remains:

> **If the absence of a violence monopolist is the prerequisite for property rights, this means that the emergence of property is only the consequence of the absence of an ultimate judge.**

Thus we have discovered the fundamental principle for solving human conflicts and thus also the root cause for the emergence of property.

### Advantages of This Perspective

This perspective has several advantages — apart from the fact that it is true:

**First**, it resolves apparent contradictions in problem areas that are even disputed among libertarians, such as the absolute validity of property rights or the controversial question of what it means for the discoverer of an ownerless plot of land to "mix" his labor with it.

**Second**, it relieves us of the necessity of debating philosophical or moral questions. State lovers are very fond of arguing about terms. Left-wing state lovers, for example, claim that it would be aggression against them if they had to work for low wages. Opponents of natural law portray it as if this were the law of the strongest. Others question the right to one's own body by saying that one is only the possessor, not the owner of one's body. There are good arguments against each of these, but if we stick to the discovered fundamental principle, the discussion of these questions is secondary.

**No one can dispute that the judge must not be part of the conflict.** Even if someone disputes it, you could say to him:

*"Fine, we have a conflict here. I propose that I decide who is right."*

If your counterpart concedes this to you, you decide that you are right. If he refuses this concession, you have proven that the conflict resolver cannot simultaneously be a party to the conflict.

That this principle precedes property rights becomes clear in another way. The state can not only take away your material property, it can gain complete control over your body. When the state imprisons and shackles you, you not only have no way to profit from the fruits of your labor, you are de facto not even the possessor of your body. You cannot move because you are shackled — for example, in a straitjacket.

It goes even further: **The state can even forbid you to argue that your body belongs to you. It can literally gag you.** In fact, it does this regularly. Immigration or Islam critics are currently being sentenced to heavy fines up to imprisonment, while at the same time foreign rapists are running around free.

The state generally prohibits "anti-state propaganda." Every anarchist is by definition an enemy of the state. The main reason why not all anarchists are in prison yet is that there are still too few of them for the state to consider them dangerous. But totalitarian regimes have always imprisoned anarchists. And the state is becoming more and more totalitarian, as even the most naive and ignorant citizen should now recognize.

**So let us note: The absence of a violence monopolist is not only a prerequisite for property, it is even a prerequisite for being allowed to argue for your right to self-ownership.**

It should now be clear that we have discovered the fundamental principle by which we can deal with all socially relevant questions of human conflicts.

# CHAPTER 3: LIBERTARIAN DISPUTES — SOME LIFEBOAT SCENARIOS

Before we get to practice, I would like to briefly address some questions that are also controversial among libertarians. State lovers like to design so-called "lifeboat" scenarios to put anarchists in argumentative difficulties. These are characterized by the fact that they are completely unrealistic and almost never occur in practice. But if I claim to have discovered the fundamental principle, I also want to defend my case using particularly hard scenarios.

## The Question of Ownerless Land

A question that is indeed controversial among libertarians is the appropriation of ownerless land. Among libertarians and all other reasonable people, it is undisputed that only the first to discover the property can acquire it conflict-free. Therefore, it belongs to the first to find it. But how large can this property be? How far do its outer boundaries extend?

**A classic lifeboat scenario:** A ship sinks and the occupants save themselves on a deserted island that doesn't belong to anyone yet. There is necessarily exactly one person who discovered the island first. Let's assume he also manages to swim to the island first. Does the whole island now belong to him? Let's also assume he had such a big head start that he very quickly pulled a rudimentary fence around the island, perhaps from ship's rope or vines.

The classic libertarian answer is: He must mix his labor with the property. It is not enough to simply pull a fence around it. So he must at least make clear that he wants to cultivate the farmland, for example, and begin to work it. But how long does he have? What percentage of the property must he cultivate by when?

I admit this question has also occupied me for a long time, and I was quite glad that state lovers so rarely dare to discuss with me. Therefore, no one had asked me this question publicly.

**But with the realization that it is not property but the absence of a violence monopolist that is decisive, the difficulties with this question dissolve into thin air.**

So let's assume the rest of the crew arrives on the island. Certainly no one would accept that the first one owns the entire island. Someone would suggest having the matter decided by a neutral person. Perhaps the captain of the ship is well-liked and they would agree on him. Perhaps the second captain or someone else on the previous voyage has proven to be particularly fair and they leave the decision to him. He would probably decide that the first one could choose a plot and the others could choose other plots in turn (either by arrival or by rank or by lot).

**Crucial: The disputants have agreed on a judge beforehand.** What basically happens when there is no agreement, I will explain later. For this scenario, it is only important to recognize: There is no absolute property right that falls magically from heaven, but it is the result of a decision-making process.

The matter becomes even clearer when we introduce the state at this point. If, for example, these were British nationals, England would certainly insist that this island now belongs to Great Britain. And as we know the state, it would probably argue that only large companies, i.e., those who already have the greatest influence on the state, would be able to develop this island. The state would grant concessions and the law firms of these companies would write the rules for them.

Whether and how much the first discoverers get of the pie is in the hands of the state. It is obvious that what comes out of this is very likely worse for the boat's occupants than what the judge appointed by the crew decides.

## The Water Source Scenario

Another scenario that — as far as I remember — once appeared in a Skype discussion between a state supporter and the anarchist Walter Block is this:

Imagine you got lost in the forest and are dying of thirst. You see a fenced property with a water source. The question the state supporter asked was: Would you drink from it?

As far as I remember, Block evaded the question and returned to fundamental questions. But the state supporter got his argument out: You would drink from it. What is a system worth if you yourself would not follow the rules (the inviolability of property)?

**My answer would have been:** The rules belong to the game, just as does the sanction for not following the rule.

**Example:** In the semi-final of the 2002 World Cup, Michael Ballack tackled a player, got a red card for it, and missed the final. Ballack knew he was risking red, but it was more valuable to him to stop the opponent. If Ballack knocked someone down on the street, he would be punished for assault. That means: **That I myself violate a rule does not mean that I reject the rule in general. But I must be willing to bear the consequences.**

Let's spin the example further: Let's assume the thirsty person climbs over the fence and drinks from the source. The owner gets his shotgun and shoots him. For simplicity, let's assume he survived and brings the owner before a neutral court. How would a neutral judge decide? We assume that someone happened to film everything.

If it were clearly visible that the owner could see that the intruder was unarmed and shot him in the back without warning, he would certainly not acquit him.

In principle, it strengthens the owner's position if he puts up signs everywhere saying that trespassing is forbidden and every intruder will be shot. But this by no means means that — without state laws — neutral, widely respected judges would allow everything.

## The Horde of Criminals Scenario

**Another example:** Let's assume a horde of criminals is about to storm your property. Your neighbor stands next to you with a gun in his hand but refuses to help you. You step onto his property, snatch the gun from him, shoot at the attackers, and fend them off. If your neighbor were to sue you in a private legal order, would a judge convict you of theft?

Certainly not. He would probably sentence you to replace the cartridges and possibly make a payment for the wear on the gun, but he would certainly not punish you for theft or for entering someone else's property.

Similarly, if you crossed this property to save a woman from rape — assuming there was no other way to reach her. If the neighbor shot you in the back while crossing his property, even though he could clearly see that you were not attacking him but only wanted to help, the neighbor would certainly be convicted.

**In reality, such scenarios are unrealistic because all parties would have voluntarily submitted to certain rules, but more on that shortly.**

My point here is to show that **property is not something absolute that somehow falls from heaven.** This is by no means to be confused with relativism, because there is an absolute rule: namely, that the judge must not be part of the conflict.

Had the shipwrecked recognized this, by the way, they would have realized that the judge is also part of the conflict because he surely would have liked to have a piece of property on the island too. The perfect solution would have been to exclude this but pay him a handsome wage for his judicial work so that he voluntarily renounces a property, or they could have promised him a certain piece of property as payment beforehand.

**In the following, I will call the underlying principle that the conflict resolver must not be a party to the conflict the *Principle of the Neutral Judge*.**

# CHAPTER 4: WHAT ABOUT THOSE WHO DON'T SUBMIT TO ANY RULES?

The question that everyone rightly asks now is: Who enforces the rules without a state? What about those who do not conclude contracts but only go around robbing and pillaging?

First, it must be noted that there are exactly **three cases** to consider. In a private legal order, competing companies would provide security. To protect their property, most people would insure themselves, but probably not all. Therefore, three cases must be distinguished when two people get into a conflict:

1. **Both are insured.** This will be the rule and is also the easiest to handle.
2. **One is insured, the other is not.**
3. **Neither is insured.**

## Case 1: Both Are Insured

When both are insured, they have previously agreed to certain rules. In principle, these rules can be reduced to exactly one: **You shall not steal.** If one considers the body as one's property, this includes not injuring or killing the other. Everything else makes no sense.

## Case 2: One Insured, One Not

There is a view, especially among voluntarists, that a murderer who has not previously signed a contract not to murder cannot be imprisoned because this would violate property rights or the non-aggression principle. I consider this view completely absurd.

Some voluntarists and also some libertarians think these people would simply be ostracized and could not move freely without insurance. This is certainly partly true, but they would still be locked up, as I will show shortly.

The reason for this view is probably precisely that the proponents of this view have not understood the root cause of the problem. They seem to believe that the observance of the non-aggression principle or property rights somehow falls from heaven. Property rights would arise because most people would believe in these principles once they have been explained to them. This assumption is, in my opinion, fundamentally wrong and also a reason why many state supporters wonder how the whole thing is supposed to work.

We see that almost no one believes in property rights because almost everyone supports the state. Now one could argue that this is because no one explains it to them. First, it is impossible to explain it to everyone, and second, the state does everything to explain it to no one. Third, there are countless people who do not accept this view even though it has been explained to them a hundred times.

**No, property rights do not arise from people's belief that they exist. They arise automatically when there is no violence monopolist.**

Let us imagine the following scenario: From one day to the next, the state abolishes itself. All insurers simultaneously come up with the idea of offering people the following contract: You can steal from whomever you want, and we support you. The insured would immediately start stealing from each other. Even the most successful thieves would be constantly robbed because it's allowed.

It is immediately obvious that this makes no sense — not even for a thief. As early as the next day, all insurers would reverse course and offer the only sensible rule: **We protect you from being robbed, and you commit not to steal.**

So we see again: Property rights arise because competing companies must offer their customers a useful product. **The violence monopolist doesn't have to. That's why he steals. Simple.**

**What about those who don't take out insurance?** Simple: They have no one to protect them. So if an uninsured thief steals from an insured person, the insurance company steps in and ensures that the thief is arrested and brought before a court.

This does not violate the non-aggression principle because the thief has already committed an aggression against the property, but that is not the decisive point. **The market does not care about abstract principles, morality, or philosophy. It simply works.**

Important for you: The abstract principle of the neutral judge does not need to be understood by anyone. This principle automatically ensures that it works. Just as disregarding the principle automatically ensures that the violence monopolist steals — precisely because he is the ultimate judge.

## Case 3: Neither Is Insured

A steals from B. Both are not insured. What can B do?

Well, B simply hires a security company that tracks down A, apprehends him, and brings him before a neutral court. The fact that B is not insured only means that he must initially pay himself. In the end, when A is convicted, A must not only compensate for the damage but also pay for the costs of his capture and conviction. More on this later.

But couldn't A also hire a security company to protect him? For this, you must understand that while it is possible as an individual not to insure yourself, **for companies it is completely impossible to operate uninsured.** An entrepreneur must hire employees, use roads, rent premises, buy weapons, and so on. For all these activities, he needs insurance. This insurer will of course require that the security company only pursues (potential) criminals and not law-abiding people who adhere to the rule of not stealing.

But couldn't A turn to a mafia company that has no insurance because it is designed to steal from everyone anyway? As I will show later, such organizations disappear from the market simply because it doesn't pay off and they cannot move within civilization anyway. Besides, I can also turn to the mafia in a state, only that this is usually identical to the state, i.e., the violence monopolist, or at least is obviously not effectively combated.

**So we see that even for the uninsured, property rights arise simply because there is no violence monopolist. And for free!** That should please socialists!

Briefly on the free-rider problem: It practically doesn't exist because it is much more economical to insure yourself. Those who still don't insure are significantly restricted in their ability to participate in civilized life.

# CHAPTER 6: NUMBERS, DATA, FACTS

If your heart sank in the previous chapters because you have to pay for security services, I can reassure you. First, nothing in life is free. Even a net transfer recipient pays — not in money, but with restrictions on his quality of life. Once in the clutches of the state, he must let himself be harassed by it. Some prefer to live on transfer payments rather than work, but they don't know the alternative either. As I calculate in my book "The Capitalism Conspiracy," we would all have had to work only a fraction of today's time long ago if the state hadn't cheated us out of the fruits of our labor through the inflationary monetary system and high taxes.

Regardless: Most people have no idea how little the state spends on the security of its citizens. **The German state spends approximately 1.6 percent of GDP** (Eurostat, 2015: 0.7% for police, 0.2% for fire department, 0.4% for courts, 0.1% for prisons, and 0.1% for public order) on internal security, i.e., for police and justice combined.

**That's only 600 euros per year, or 50 euros per month per capita.**

Nobel laureate Milton Friedman once estimated that by dissolving a monopoly, prices roughly halve. **That would be only 25 euros per month.**

Even a beggar pays more in taxes today. While he doesn't pay taxes on his income, with every purchase he pays not only VAT but also the taxes of the company from which he buys his products, the taxes and social contributions of the employees, the insurance tax for the building of the store, the eco and energy tax on electricity, and so on and so forth.

If a beggar thus takes in about ten euros a day and spends it again, certainly 50 percent of it, i.e., 150 euros per month, flows to the state. **An employee pays about 70 percent of his income in all taxes and social contributions combined.**

The German state spends about another one percent, i.e., 30 euros per month per citizen, on the military. In a global private legal order, there would be no need for military spending, but even if there were still states, private defense costs would be much lower, as I will show.

But to be safe, let's assume in the following that the costs for security would also be 80 euros per month in a private legal order. **Almost everyone can afford this too**, and no one would be forced to buy security services.

What remains is the fee for using the roads. Here, too, there is the big misconception that the state provides them free of charge. Far from it.

**An example:** For the 776-kilometer drive from Milan to Naples, the private highway operator Autostrade charges 56 euros. If you apply the average fuel consumption of 7.9 liters per kilometer, you consume 61 liters of gasoline. At a gasoline price of 152 cents per liter, 90 cents per liter goes as direct taxes to the state, which at 55 euros corresponds pretty exactly to the price of the highway provider. In addition, there is car tax, insurance tax, taxes when buying the car, taxes and contributions of the employees who produce the car and the road, and so on.

Moreover, this is not even a free market price here because the state guarantees Autostrade an exclusive monopoly.

Highways in a private legal order are also not comparable to so-called public-private partnerships (PPP). These are often a disaster because the state always has a say and is allowed to squander taxpayer money (see also Berlin Airport). When purely private providers go bankrupt, the investor bears the losses. The road can be sold and a more efficient company then operates it.

In addition, most roads would be "free" in the sense that they would be paid for by residents. In private cities or so-called gated communities, the costs for roads are simply factored into real estate prices. In addition, there is usually an annual maintenance fee that gets lost in the utility costs.

So if you visit someone in such an area, the road is free for you. And of course, the quality is much higher. **Just compare any private roads with public ones: a difference like night and day.** The reason for this is, of course, that the providers want to attract customers, whether for an interregional road or for a residential project.

# CHAPTER 7: ANARCHY IN PRACTICE — THE RETURN OF BOUNTY HUNTERS

In a private legal order, profit-oriented companies would take care of security. It is important to understand that the market tends toward division of labor and specialization.

For example, there are only four major car manufacturers in Germany but hundreds of suppliers who each focus on what they do best. It would be similar in the security industry.

While it would be theoretically conceivable for a corporation to simultaneously offer insurance against theft and the security team that guards your house or catches criminals, it is extremely unlikely that this will prevail because significant conflicts of interest arise here (moral hazard).

An insurer has an interest, for example, in not paying out the insurance sum. If the same company also conducts the investigations, there would be a temptation to manipulate the investigation results so that the payout amount is as low as possible. So if, for example, the insurance sum is low or — as is usually the case — zero in the case of suicide, there is a tendency to declare a murder as suicide. The state does this today too because then it doesn't have to work and can embellish the statistics.

Another problem is coordinating all the security corporations. What if the criminal flees to a territory where the corporation is not or only poorly represented?

How would you solve this problem if you were an entrepreneur?

## The Solution: Rewards

In economic action, indeed in every action, it's about incentives. So what would an incentive look like that solves the above problems?

**The solution lies in one word, which is really just another expression for incentive: Reward.**

In the not-so-wild West (see "The Not So Wild Wild West"), the problem of apprehending criminals was efficiently solved by placing a bounty on them. The state also occasionally offers rewards for useful information today.

**But this system could be applied not only to the capture of perpetrators but also to the investigation of crimes.** I propose the following method:

A insures himself against murder. When he dies, a certain sum is automatically made available for solving the case. This money is paid to whoever solves the crime, should it turn out to be one. The payout is decided by a company independent of the insurer, which A determines beforehand.

Since only a vanishingly small proportion of deaths have murder as a cause, this insurance would be very cheap.

**A numerical example:**

A term life insurance policy for one million euros for a twenty-year-old with a twenty-year term costs about 25 euros per month (see comparison portals). Of 925,000 deaths in Germany in 2015, only 373 were classified as murder. The number is certainly too low because the state has an incentive to assume a natural cause of death because then it doesn't have to investigate.

But you can already see that with so few cases, it doesn't carry much weight. Even if one were to consider all 10,000 suicides as murder, the numbers would be very low.

If we assumed ten thousand murders, an insurance against murder would cost one percent (10,000/925,000) of 25 euros, which would be **0.25 euros**. Taking the official murder rate, we arrive at **one cent per month!**

So you see, we're talking about ridiculous amounts. You could also make ten million euros available for investigations. Then, according to official figures, it would be ten cents, according to actual murders perhaps twice as much.

**How much does the state spend today on solving crimes?** In 2016, 6.4 million crimes were recorded. For police and justice, the state spends 1.6 percent of GDP of 3.1 trillion euros, i.e., almost 50 billion euros, which is 7,750 euros per crime. For murder investigation (and complex economic crimes), certainly the most is spent per case.

But you can already see: **Ten million euros per murder is plenty.**

## The Power of the Reward System

Through the reward system, however, an interesting effect occurs that leads to much more being spent than the offered sum. From an econometric standpoint, one would assume that the sum of expenditures at the end roughly corresponds to the offered reward, minus the industry-standard return.

But this is only an idealized model. In practice, newcomers always enter the market who, for example, want to make a name for themselves by solving a case. They might even be willing to spend more than ten million just for the publicity.

It's hard to say where the optimal point lies. Private detective agencies might spend up to ten percent to get the total sum. Companies that regularly miscalculate drop out of the market.

**But the decisive thing is: The ten million (or whatever sum) always remains in the fire**, or for as long as previously agreed. The premium is then based on that. But since we're talking about penny amounts, the term of the contract will be as long as possible.

So let's assume a number of detective agencies have spent money and at some point everyone gives up because the indications of a crime are not solidifying. Suddenly, a new clue appears. Part of the money would always be made available for useful tips from the public, so there should be no shortage of informants. A new witness could appear, a forged suicide note, a false alibi, and so on.

**No matter how much was spent before, suddenly the incentive is there to continue investigating.**

The agencies that had already spent a lot of money now have the incentive to save their investment and possess a knowledge advantage through the information already collected. They could also resell this information to recoup part of their investments. But a newcomer could also start from scratch, because with this new clue, the risk/reward ratio has significantly improved.

**Compare that to today's situation.** Today, a single overworked prosecutor decides the case. The investigation is conducted by poorly paid police officers with overtime who have not the slightest economic interest in the case. They are unsackable, and if they don't commit a crime themselves, they keep their job even if they take it easy.

This is not to say that there are no police officers who don't put in the effort anyway. But the frustration, especially of police officers, is growing thanks to politics. When they do catch a criminal and it's a migrant, he's usually released immediately anyway or gets a ridiculously light sentence.

**The reward system also explains who captures the perpetrators.** Part of the money is always offered for the capture of the perpetrator. So every security company in a private legal order has an interest in apprehending criminals. In addition, they of course want to protect their customers because otherwise they lose their contracts. Nothing would be more embarrassing for a security company than if a wanted criminal is in their area and then robs a customer.

**Now put yourself in the position of a criminal today and tomorrow.**

In which world would you rather be a criminal?

- **Today:** An overworked, poorly paid detective is responsible for you, and the penalties are ridiculously low.

- **Tomorrow:** Hundreds of detective agencies are hunting you to get the reward placed on your head.

**I think you would quickly hang up your "job" as a criminal.**

## Benefits for the Poor

Another aspect is that especially poor people would benefit enormously from such a system.

Let's take a beggar as an extreme case. He doesn't need to insure against theft because he lives from hand to mouth. If someone steals his collection bowl, he has to beg longer the next day, not eat for a day, or go to a food bank.

He may also have no great interest in insuring against murder because as a beggar, he already has a certain fatalism. In a civilized world where no Islamists run around simply slaughtering "infidels," there is hardly an economic reason to kill him.

But private charities could have an interest in insuring him. Since this costs only a few cents, they could insure him in addition to the soup they give him. He would only have to leave a fingerprint, for example.

**The interesting thing is:** If he dies, suddenly there is an economic interest in solving the case. Today, neither the police nor the public care much when a beggar dies. But a private detective agency does, because they get the reward no matter who it is.

The charity also has an interest in providing useful tips because then part of the reward beckons, which in turn can be spent on charitable purposes.

**The beggar is only an extreme case.** In general, poor people have no big lobby. But since the protection is so cheap, it is guaranteed that private detective agencies will also take care of solving such cases.

**Ten million euros is ten million euros.**

### Potential Conflicts of Interest

If you're a crime fiction fan, you'll probably immediately think that this would be an incentive to have the beggar murdered and then solve the case to collect the reward. But the reward is only paid out if the murderer is caught, and he would certainly be the first to sing — in exchange for a lighter sentence — about who gave him the contract. Furthermore, the insurance company has an interest in not paying out and would conduct counter-investigations if something seems suspicious to them.

But fundamentally, it is very important to examine what conflicts of interest would arise in such a system and how they can be avoided.
